Analysis report

Securities lending exposure in US funds

Who borrows securities from US mutual funds and ETFs, how concentrated that borrowing has become, and whether the collateral behind it holds up.

Prepared by Ankit Singathia
Source SEC Form N-PORT, four quarterly postings
Portfolios dated May 2025 to Apr 2026
Issued 26 September 2026

The question

Funds lend the shares and bonds they hold to banks, for a fee. The loans are collateralised and the borrower is named in every filing, yet the exposure is only ever disclosed one fund at a time. Nobody adds it up. This report does, for every US mutual fund and ETF that filed, and asks three things: who is on the other side, how concentrated is it, and is it covered.

What the filings show

  • $339.0bn is on loan from 4,109 of 12,944 funds in the latest posting, to 58 bank groups.
  • Five banks take 58.4% of it. Barclays (14.6%), JPMorgan Chase (12.6%), Morgan Stanley (11.9%), Goldman Sachs (11.0%), Citigroup (8.3%).
  • Collateral covers 103.1% of loans, and the genuine shortfall is $1.6bn, not the $39.5bn a literal reading of the filings suggests.
  • 17 funds lend a third or more of their net assets, and four of them do it in every posting.

What we recommend

  • Monitor the four funds that sit at 43 to 47% of net assets in every posting.
  • Review the 49 funds below 90% cover, and ask GMO where the collateral for about $0.5bn of loans is held.
  • Measure counterparty exposure at parent-group level, not by identifier, or JPMorgan splits across a London entity and a Paris branch.

01Why this is worth measuring

Securities lending is routine and, in normal weather, dull. A fund lends stock to a bank, the bank posts collateral worth slightly more than the loan, the fund earns a few basis points and the collateral is marked daily. The risk only appears in two places: if the borrower fails at the same moment the collateral falls short, and if a lot of funds happen to face the same borrower at once. Both are invisible in any single filing, which is what makes the aggregate worth building.

Form N-PORT is the raw material. Every registered fund files its portfolio monthly, the securities it has out on loan, the name and Legal Entity Identifier of each borrower, and the collateral held against them. Four quarterly postings were read here, May 2025 to Apr 2026, covering 12,944 funds in the latest one.

02What had to be fixed before anything could be added up

N-PORT is published exactly as funds submit it. The filings are not audited and not standardised, and the first pass at the data is wrong in ways that are easy to miss. Five problems mattered enough to change the numbers:

03Five banks borrow most of what US funds lend

Borrowers are grouped to their ultimate parent, so a bank's London entity, its Paris branch and its US broker-dealer count as one counterparty. On that basis 58 groups borrow from US funds, and the top five take 58.4% of the total. The market is not dominated by a single firm, the HHI of 864 sits below the 1,000 mark regulators treat as unconcentrated, but more than half of every dollar goes to five names.

04Lending grew 23% over the year, and the leaders moved

Across the four postings the amount on loan rose from $275.7bn to $339.0bn, up 23%. Concentration barely moved with it: the top five share went from 57.5% to 58.4%. Growth came from both directions, 495 funds lending for the first time in the latest posting and adding $14.6bn, and existing lenders adding the rest.

Underneath the flat total, the order changed. JPMorgan Chase gained the most over the year, +1.9 points, while Bank of America lost 1.5. Click a name in the legend to isolate it.

05Most funds lend a little, a few lend to the limit

The median lending fund has 1.7% of its net assets out on loan. The 99th percentile is 27.8%. SEC guidance caps lending at about a third of total assets, which is roughly half of net assets once collateral is counted, so the shape of this distribution matters more than its average: 17 funds sit at or above a third of net assets in the latest posting.

Concentration repeats inside the funds. The median fund places 51.0% of its lending with a single bank group, and the largest single exposure is Quantified Tactical Sectors Fund, which has 50.9% of its net assets lent to TD Securities (Usa) LLC alone.

06Cover is sound overall, and the real gaps are small

Funds hold $270.1bn of reinvested cash collateral and $79.3bn of non-cash collateral, mostly US Treasuries, against $339.0bn of loans. That is 103.1% cover. Read literally, 572 funds with $39.5bn on loan are below 100%, which sounds alarming and is mostly an artefact: loans are re-marked daily and three quarters of that value sits within three points of full cover. Summing only what is actually missing gives $1.6bn, concentrated in 49 funds below 90%.

The shape is the point. Funds lending heavily, on the right, sit tightly on full cover. The wide spread is all among funds lending very little, where a single lumpy collateral position moves the ratio. Thin cover and heavy lending do not coincide.

07Cash collateral pools into a handful of vehicles

Cash collateral is not held idle, it is reinvested, and funds overwhelmingly reinvest into the same few money market vehicles. That is a second, quieter concentration: a problem in one vehicle would reach hundreds of funds at once, whoever their borrowers were.

08Funds carrying the same problem in every posting

A fund near the ceiling in one quarter is noise. The same fund there in all four is a policy. Seven funds qualify, and they split into two unrelated groups: four that lend heavily but are fully covered, and three whose filings never show most of their collateral.

10How this was produced

The quarterly data sets are loaded into DuckDB with every column as text, so nothing is coerced on the way in. Six SQL steps resolve borrowers to parent groups, keep one snapshot per fund, measure exposure and collateral, and compare the postings. Fifty-five tests run those same SQL files against small quarters built with planted faults, a wrong identifier, a duplicate filing, a placeholder identifier, a filing that contradicts itself, so the rules are checked rather than assumed. Every number in this report is queried at build time; none is typed in.

11What changed during the analysis

Five findings in earlier drafts were wrong and were corrected against the data. They are kept in the decision log because the corrections are part of the evidence:

  • A correction rule applied in the wrong order moved $19.6bn between two JPMorgan entities and reported $27.8bn of wrong identifiers. Checking the name before correcting fixed it.
  • An early version used the fiscal year end rather than the portfolio date, which can sit in the future.
  • A claimed tagging gap at GMO was contradicted by the funds' own numbers; the honest statement is that the collateral is not visible.
  • A claim that no fund added more than $1.8bn was false: one new lender added $3.4bn.
  • Collateral totals were once quoted on a different scope from the cover ratio beside them.

12Limitations

  • Filings are not audited. Every figure is as reported by the fund.
  • A posting is not a calendar quarter. Each holds funds' latest month-end portfolios, so the newest one covers Feb 2026 to Apr 2026. Every figure here is labelled with its holdings window.
  • Cash collateral is measured at reinvested value. Cover below 100% can mean too little was taken or that a reinvestment lost value, and the filing cannot separate the two.
  • Exposure is gross. Positions offsetting between a fund and the same bank elsewhere are not visible in N-PORT.
