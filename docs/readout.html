Who borrows from US funds, and is it safe?

Ankit Singathia · SEC Form N-PORT, four postings, portfolios dated May 2025 to Apr 2026

Every US mutual fund and ETF files its full portfolio with the SEC, including the securities it lends out and who borrows them. I read four quarterly postings of those filings. In the latest, 4,109 of 12,944 funds were lending, with $339.0bn on loan to 58 bank groups, backed by collateral worth 103.1% of the loans.

The short version

Five banks borrow most of it: Barclays (14.6%), JPMorgan Chase (12.6%), Morgan Stanley (11.9%), Goldman Sachs (11.0%), Citigroup (8.3%). Together that is 58.4% of all lending. The market as a whole is not dominated by one firm, but more than half of every dollar goes to those five.

Most funds lend very little: the median lending fund has 1.7% of its assets out. A few lend right up to the limit, and four of them do it in every posting I looked at. Collateral covers 103.1% of loans overall, and once daily re-marking is allowed for, the real shortfall is $1.6bn, not the $39.5bn a literal reading suggests.

Before trusting any of it

The filings are published exactly as funds submit them, and it shows. Before I added anything up, I had to fix four things:

  1. The same bank is filed under many names. Morgan Stanley alone appears eleven ways. I matched names to each firm's Legal Entity Identifier and checked every identifier against GLEIF, the official registry. In the latest posting 99.6% of the value is backed by an identifier the registry agrees with.
  2. Some identifiers belong to a different bank: a JPMorgan name filed with UBS's identifier, for example. Those are corrected when the name is confirmed elsewhere and flagged when it is not. None is moved silently.
  3. Many funds file twice in a posting, with a late filing for an earlier month or an amendment. Adding everything up gives $383.6bn; counting each fund once gives $339.0bn. The difference, $44.6bn, is double counting.
  4. Each filing reports its lending twice, once per borrower and once per position. For 99.7% of the value the two agree, and I only rank funds where they do.

Who borrows

Over the year JPMorgan Chase gained the most share (+1.9 points) and Bank of America lost the most (-1.5 points). Barclays led in every posting.

How much each fund lends

Lending is uneven. The median fund has 1.7% of its net assets on loan; one in a hundred has 27.8% or more. SEC guidance caps lending at a third of total assets, and because the collateral received counts as an asset, that works out to about half of net assets. Half of lending funds also place 51% or more of their lending with a single bank group.

Collateral

Funds hold $270.1bn of reinvested cash and $79.3bn of non-cash collateral, mostly US Treasuries. Read literally, 572 funds with $39.5bn on loan are under 100% cover. But loans are re-marked every day and a filing freezes prices between marks, so most of that is a point or two of normal drift. The actual gap is $1.6bn, and the funds worth a look are the 49 below 90%.

What I got wrong along the way

Four of my own mistakes changed a number before I caught them, and each is written up in the decision log with how I found it:

  1. A matching rule moved $19.6bn of genuine JPMorgan lending between two JPMorgan entities and reported $27.8bn of "wrong identifiers" that were right. I found it by reading the largest corrections before trusting the total.
  2. I first used a fund's fiscal year end as the date of its portfolio. The two columns look alike; one can be in the future.
  3. I explained GMO's missing collateral as untagged short-term holdings. The numbers did not support it, so the write-up now says only what the filing shows.
  4. I claimed no single fund drove the last posting's rise by more than $1.8bn. A fund lending for the first time added $3.4bn.
